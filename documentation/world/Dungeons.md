# Dungeons

## Overview

A dungeon is a spatial anomaly: a pocket of space that does not quite obey the geometry of the land around it, sealed behind a boundary nothing yet made — steel, powder, or spell — has been able to breach from the outside. To the eye a dungeon entrance can look like almost anything the local theme demands: a crack in a glacier, a collapsed cellar beneath a burned-out manor, a gate in a wall that was not there the week before. What lies behind the entrance is never as small as it looks from the front.

Dungeons are not common. Where a mine or a quarry can be found wherever the geology cooperates, a dungeon requires a standing concentration of ambient mana far beyond what most regions ever accumulate, and such concentrations are themselves rare and slow to build. A working dungeon is consequently a landmark in its own right, known by name to the scholars, chartered companies, and governments with a claim on the ground it sits on, and watched by all three.

## The Barrier

The moment a group crosses a dungeon's threshold, the entrance closes behind them — not physically, but behind an invisible wall that resists every method yet tried against it, from siege engines to modern ordnance to the most aggressive battle magic on record. Nothing has cut it, burned it, or forced it open from outside. It yields, when it yields at all, only from within: the barrier lifts on its own once the party inside has died, retreated to the entrance, or cleared the dungeon outright. Only small groups can pass through in the first place, which has done more than any regulation to keep dungeon delving a professional trade rather than an army's errand — there is no way to send an army in behind the first party regardless.

This has made rescue, reinforcement, and observation from outside a dungeon impossible in every recorded case. What happens to a party once the wall closes is known only through what that party reports afterward, if it reports anything at all.

## Layers and Danger

Dungeons are organized into layers, each one typically harder than the last, and the layer count is the roughest and most widely used measure of how dangerous a given dungeon is likely to be. A single-layer dungeon might be a reasonable proving ground for a licensed adventuring band; a dungeon known to run eight or ten layers deep is the kind of site that draws chartered companies, standing rewards, and a body count. The correlation is not exact — a shallow dungeon can still kill an unprepared party outright, and depth alone says little about what a party will actually face — but it holds well enough that layer count is the first number quoted whenever a new dungeon is surveyed.

## Themes and Denizens

No two dungeons are populated at random. Each one settles into and holds a coherent theme — an ice cavern, the buried remains of a ruined city, the halls of a classical stone castle, and stranger variations besides — and the monsters, terrain, and hazards inside are drawn to match it. A frost-themed dungeon does not produce fire elementals; a ruined-city dungeon does not suddenly sprout a throne room. What generates the theme in the first place, and why a given site settles on one motif rather than another, is one of the many questions dungeon scholarship has not answered.

## Repopulation

A dungeon that has been fully cleared does not stay empty. Within anywhere from a day to a month, depending on the site, the layers refill: monsters return, hazards reset, and the dungeon is once again fit to delve. No one has observed the repopulation happening — parties that return early simply find the dungeon full again, with no record of how or when the transition occurred. This cycle is what turns a given dungeon from a one-time expedition into a standing resource, and most of the trade built around dungeons — licensing, scheduling, rival companies racing to be first back in after a clear — exists because of it.

## An Open Question of Mind

The central controversy in dungeon scholarship is whether a dungeon is a purely natural phenomenon or something closer to a creature. The case for the latter is circumstantial but hard to dismiss: dungeons have been observed to shift their own internal layout between visits, to adjust which monsters populate a layer, and — most tellingly — to escalate in difficulty specifically in response to the parties that keep beating them. A dungeon that has repeatedly fallen to a particular weapon, tactic, or type of party will, over subsequent cycles, rework its layers, its monsters, or its terrain until that approach no longer trivializes it, holding its overall difficulty roughly level against whatever its visitors have learned to do to it.

Whether this amounts to sentience, some lesser semi-aware instinct, or an entirely mechanical process that only resembles adaptation is genuinely unsettled. No one has ever established two-way communication with a dungeon, or found anything inside one that behaves like a mind to bargain with rather than a mechanism to survive. Scholars who argue for sentience point to the specificity of the adjustments; skeptics point out that nothing rules out a self-regulating natural system that adapts the way a wound scars, without anything present that could be called awareness. The debate has run since dungeons were first catalogued and shows no sign of resolving soon.

## Rewards

Every dungeon produces chests, and every chest is worth the risk in a way that keeps the trade alive despite everything above. What a dungeon yields, however, is bounded in a very particular way: chests hold raw materials only — magical ores, gemstones, enchanted timber, and other such substances — never crafted goods, tools, or finished items of any kind. The materials themselves are not unique to dungeons; most can occasionally be found in nature. What a dungeon offers is density and reliability: the same conditions of high mana concentration that allow a dungeon to form at all are the conditions under which these materials occur naturally, so a chest is, in effect, a guaranteed sample of something the surrounding land might produce only once in a generation, if ever.

## Notes / open threads for future development

- The formation mechanism is deliberately left unresolved in-universe; competing scholarly schools (natural mana phenomenon vs. latent Ankarene/divine remnant vs. something else entirely) could be a good vehicle for exposition without committing the setting to a single answer.
- The barrier's total imperviousness to modern weapons is a strong hook for why dungeons remain relevant even as Earth technology on Ankaris modernizes — worth flagging anywhere tech progression or military events are being designed, so writers don't accidentally imply an entrance can be breached from outside.
- The escalating-difficulty response to repeated tactics could be tied to a game mechanic (e.g., a decay on repeatedly using the same strategy against a given dungeon), which would also reinforce the sentience ambiguity mechanically rather than just narratively.
- Worth deciding later whether different dungeon themes cluster with particular regions/races (e.g., ice-cave dungeons more common near dwarven or northern territories) or whether theme is fully independent of location — currently unspecified.
- Chest rewards being restricted to raw materials only (no crafted items) has economic implications worth threading into trade/market design: dungeons feed the crafting economy as an ore/gem supply, they don't compete with it.
- No named or catalogued individual dungeons yet — a first concrete example (with a specific theme, layer count, and location) would help ground this document and give other docs something to reference.
